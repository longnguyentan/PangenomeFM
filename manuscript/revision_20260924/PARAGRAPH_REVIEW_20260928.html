Human pangenome graphs represent alternative sequence arrangements through connections among shared segments. Learning from these connections requires both genomic position and the structure of alternative routes through the graph. We introduce PangenomeFM, a self-supervised model that combines information from nearby genomic positions and connected segments through a learned gate. Pretraining predicts graph connections without biological labels. The segment representations then remain fixed when used to classify known structural variants and regulatory elements or predict locus-level susceptibility to allele-specific activity. Evaluation on held-out chromosomes compares these representations with genomic features and independently pretrained nucleotide embeddings. Graph statistics and untrained encoders provide controls for separating the contribution of graph structure from that of pretraining. The study asks whether native pangenome connectivity provides reusable biological information beyond sequence, and what learning adds to simpler graph representations.Human pangenome graphs describe shared and alternative sequences through their connections. These connections place each segment in a structural context that is absent from a linear sequence alone. We introduce PangenomeFM, a self-supervised model for learning from this context. The encoder combines attention over nearby genomic positions with attention over connected segments through a learned gate and is pretrained to recover hidden connections. Its representations remain frozen during biological evaluation, alongside independently pretrained nucleotide embeddings. We evaluate structural-variant classification, regulatory-element annotation and susceptibility to allele-specific activity on held-out chromosomes. Comparisons with genomic features, sequence embeddings, graph statistics and untrained encoders assess both the predictive value of graph structure and the contribution of pretraining. Pangenome topology describes where alternative routes branch, where they rejoin and how their segments are oriented. Segments with similar nucleotide composition can occupy different structural contexts, while structurally similar regions can contain divergent sequences. The graph also defines a neighbourhood distinct from genomic proximity. Alternative alleles can occupy similar reference positions without being directly connected, whereas the flanks of a deletion can be distant in reference coordinates but adjacent in the graph. A representation must accommodate both relationships and their orientations. Aggregate connectivity alone does not identify which donor follows each route, however; individual allelic interpretation additionally requires verified haplotype paths.Pangenome topology describes relationships that genomic coordinates and nucleotide composition do not specify. Genomic proximity and graph adjacency also need not coincide: alternative alleles may occupy similar reference positions without being directly connected, whereas the flanks of a deletion can be far apart in reference coordinates but adjacent in the graph. Learning from a pangenome requires a representation of both relationships. Aggregate connectivity describes the alternatives present at a locus; identifying the route followed by an individual additionally requires verified haplotype paths.

Existing approaches introduce pangenome information at different stages of learning (Fig. 1). DeepGene converts graph-derived information into sequence input[21]. PangenomeX uses a phylogeny-guided network of copy-number relationships among individuals for supervised prediction[22]. Learning directly from native segment connectivity without biological labels poses a different problem. Graph attention[23] and reconstruction of connections or masked node features[24, 25, 26] provide established building blocks for this task. In link prediction, positive and negative sampling can introduce cues that inflate performance[27, 28], a form of shortcut learning[29].
