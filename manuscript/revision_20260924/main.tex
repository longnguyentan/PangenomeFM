\begin{abstract}
Human pangenome graphs represent alternative sequence arrangements and the connections among them. Learning from these connections requires a representation that retains both genomic position and the structure of alternative routes through the graph. We introduce PangenomeFM, a self-supervised model that combines information from nearby genomic positions and connected graph segments through a learned gate at each layer. Pretraining predicts graph connections without biological labels. The resulting segment representations remain fixed when used to classify known structural-variant types and regulatory elements or to predict locus-level susceptibility to allele-specific activity. Evaluation on held-out chromosomes compares these representations with genomic features and independently pretrained nucleotide embeddings. Additional comparisons with graph statistics and random-weight encoders test what information graph structure supplies and what pretraining adds. The study addresses two related questions: whether native pangenome connectivity provides reusable biological information beyond sequence, and whether learning from that connectivity yields a benefit beyond simpler graph representations.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} human pangenome; graph representation learning; self-supervision; structural variation; regulatory genomics

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% Long Note: Retain the six-paragraph introduction requested in the original
% comments. Following Examples 1/2, move from the scientific gap to the design
% constraint and then to the tests; do not substitute a list of datasets for novelty.

Human genome representations determine which relationships an analysis can express. Linear references, including GRCh38 and the telomere-to-telomere CHM13 assembly, provide coordinate systems for comparing genomes and locating biological annotations \citep{schneider2017grch38,nurk2022complete}. A single reference sequence, however, cannot directly represent all alternative sequence arrangements in a population. Human pangenome graphs connect shared segments to alternative sequences, bringing this variation into one representation. Haplotype-resolved assemblies from the Human Pangenome Reference Consortium (HPRC) and Human Genome Structural Variation Consortium (HGSVC) have made such graphs increasingly practical \citep{hprc2023draft,hgsvc2021,hgsvc32025}. A central challenge is to learn from the relationships among their segments while retaining the genomic positions needed to connect them to biological annotations.

% Long Note: Done [R02]. Macias-Velasco et al. is cited with DOI 10.1038/s41467-026-73663-3;
% its genome/pipeline scope is stated accurately.
This problem matters for both structural and functional genomics. Assembly-based pangenomes improve access to structurally diverse loci and support variant discovery and genotyping \citep{hprc2023draft,hgsvc32025}. Genome choice also affects functional-genomics measurements and their interpretation. Macias-Velasco et al. compared reference, personal and pangenome representations across accessibility, expression, methylation and chromatin-contact assays, showing that their effects depend on the analysis pipeline as well as the represented sequence \citep{maciasvelasco2026benchmarking}. Representing more variation does not, by itself, determine how that information should be used for prediction. A learned representation must preserve relevant relationships and be evaluated against the sequence and genomic features already available for each locus. Structural variants and regulatory annotations offer complementary settings for this comparison.

Genomic foundation models learn reusable features from large sequence collections. DNABERT and Nucleotide Transformer encode nucleotide context for regulatory and variant-related prediction \citep{ji2021dnabert,dalla2025nucleotide}. Their training sequences can contain population diversity, but an individual sequence embedding---a numerical representation of a nucleotide sequence---does not explicitly describe how alternative segments connect in a pangenome. Graph branches mark alternative routes, and reconverging branches show where those routes rejoin. Segments with similar nucleotide composition can therefore occupy different structural contexts, while structurally similar regions can contain divergent sequences. Whether these relationships provide predictive information beyond nucleotide sequence requires a direct comparison.

Such a comparison must distinguish two forms of local context. Genomic proximity connects loci along a reference coordinate system; graph adjacency connects segments through the represented sequence arrangements. Neither fully describes the other. Representing each segment with its orientation preserves the direction of these connections, while combining positional and graph processing allows the model to use both forms of context. The aggregate structural-variation (SV) graph considered here represents relationships among segments without retaining the paths taken by individual donors. Its segment representations are therefore designed for locus-level analysis, rather than for distinguishing a person's two alleles.

Related approaches use pangenomes at different stages of learning (\cref{fig:paradigms}). DeepGene converts graph-derived information into sequence representations \citep{zhang2025deepgene}, whereas PangenomeX uses a phylogeny-guided copy-number relationship network for supervised prediction \citep{xue2025pangenomex}. Learning directly from native segment connections, without biological labels, offers another approach. The resulting representations can be held fixed and evaluated across tasks, with only the downstream classifier trained on each task's labels. This requires two distinct comparisons: against coordinate and sequence features to measure the graph's additional contribution, and against simple graph statistics and random-weight encoders to measure what pretraining adds.

Here we introduce \method to learn reusable representations from native human pangenome connectivity. The model combines information from nearby genomic positions with information from connected segments, using a learned gate to weight these contributions at each layer. It is trained to predict masked graph connections, after which its segment representations remain fixed. We evaluate known SV types, candidate cis-regulatory elements and EN-TEx allele-specific activity on chromosomes excluded from downstream training. Comparisons with sequence features, graph statistics and random-weight encoders are designed to separate the information supplied by the graph from the contribution of pretraining. External evaluations further test the scope of reuse. The methodological focus is the representation of native pangenome connections and their integration with genomic position; the attention, gating and optimization components build on established methods.

\subsection{Graph context informs the classification of known SV types}
\label{sec:sv-result}

For insertion-versus-deletion classification among known SV records, frozen PangenomeFM representations ($T$) improved prediction beyond genomic coordinate and structural features ($C$) combined with frozen Nucleotide Transformer embeddings ($S$). Area under the precision--recall curve (AUPRC) increased from 0.8721 for $C+S$ to 0.9063 with graph context restricted to the same region (strict) and 0.9122 with directly connected segments included (one-hop). The paired gains were 0.0342 (\CI 0.0324--0.0362) and 0.0401 (0.0361--0.0440), respectively, across five held-out chromosome folds and three archived executions (\cref{fig:sv-result}). Only the downstream classifier was trained on SV labels. The one-hop-minus-strict difference in gain was 0.0058 (0.0033--0.0084). Conversely, adding $S$ to $C+T$ yielded changes of $-0.0002$ and $-0.0005$, with intervals spanning zero. These comparisons concern the evaluated segment features and event representation; they do not imply that sequence is generally uninformative for SVs.

Gains persisted across allele-frequency groups and adequately powered length strata. Approximately 27 examples per run in the 100-kb--1-Mb stratum produced uncertain negative estimates, which remain reported (\cref{tab:s-sv-length}). Complexity did not amplify the SV gain: strict estimates were 0.0419, 0.0380 and 0.0302 in low, medium and high groups, and one-hop estimates were 0.0499, 0.0474 and 0.0336 (\cref{fig:sv-complexity}). Donor stratification excluding five overlapping donors gave 60-donor macro gains of 0.0367/0.0430. The probes still used those donors' labels on other chromosomes, so this is not donor-held-out fitting (\cref{fig:s-donor}).

\subsection{Regulatory reuse depends on the biological endpoint}
\label{sec:ccre-result}

For candidate cis-regulatory element (cCRE) classification, $C+S$ reached AUPRC 0.9185; adding $T$ increased it to 0.9225 under strict context and 0.9219 under one-hop context. Paired gains were 0.0040 (\CI 0.0029--0.0048) and 0.0034 (0.0017--0.0048), respectively (\cref{fig:ccre-result,tab:downstream}). Sequence supplied the larger conditional contribution, adding 0.0248 and 0.0261 beyond $C+T$ (\cref{tab:s-factorial}). The graph encoder remained fixed throughout these comparisons. The feature gains therefore demonstrate reuse across tasks, but do not by themselves establish that trained weights outperform random graph encoders.

Subsetting the same binary probe's predictions gave strict gains of 0.0058 for promoter-like elements, 0.0209 for proximal enhancers, 0.0054 for distal enhancers and 0.0349 for CTCF-only elements versus common background. These are subtype evaluations, not separately trained classifiers. Native low-, medium- and high-complexity cCRE gains were 0.0018, 0.0014 and 0.0076; that pattern is specific to this annotation task.

For measured regulatory activity in EN-TEx \citep{rozowsky2023entex}, the contribution of $T$ varied by endpoint. Prediction of cCREs prone to allele-specific (AS) activity and active-versus-repressed distal enhancers, averaged equally across tissues, was inconclusive in both contexts. CTCF allele-specific single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) showed small positive gains: 0.00147 (\CI 0.00010--0.00355) strict and 0.00225 (0.00096--0.00348) one-hop. H3K27ac had a positive interval only in one-hop context, and H3K4me3 had positive intervals in both contexts. RNA allele-specific expression, chromatin accessibility measured by ATAC and H3K27me3 remained inconclusive. These intervals apply to individual comparisons and are not adjusted across the assay panel. The shared segment vectors describe locus susceptibility, rather than differences between an individual's two alleles. The full panel and the exposure-matched and assay-specific sensitivity analyses remain in \cref{tab:s-entex}; multiple comparisons and low absolute AP limit a broader regulatory claim.

\subsubsection{Sequence coverage is complete, but long-segment context is restricted}

The fixed Nucleotide Transformer pipeline produced 512-dimensional vectors for all 303,425 segments required by the cCRE and insertion/deletion classifiers. \subsection{Structural information and pretraining make different contributions}
\label{sec:controls-result}

The trained encoder did not consistently outperform a random-weight encoder once the same coordinate, sequence and graph-statistic features were included. Across the 120-run comparison, conditioning on $C+S+H$, where $H$ contains 14 label-free graph statistics, left a trained-minus-random AP difference of 0.005295 (\CI 0.002787--0.010515) for strict INS/DEL classification (\cref{tab:s-hr}). The corresponding one-hop interval included zero. Strict cCRE favored random weights, and one-hop cCRE was inconclusive. No AP contrast passed the exact fold-level test with BH correction. Evidence for a benefit from learned weights is thus confined to an individual task and context before correction for multiple comparisons. The full-graph $H$ features also use a broader neighborhood than the regional $T$ vectors.

The connection-reconstruction objective provides a possible explanation for this limited advantage. Hiding a true connection reduces the number of visible connections at its endpoints, termed their degree; endpoints of matched negative pairs need not lose a connection. The original input features also retain degree and component labels from the unmasked graph. A simple product of endpoint degrees, known as preferential attachment, reached strict AUPRC 0.9835, and their sum reached 0.9742, exceeding the original model. These findings qualify the reconstruction scores; the reversal in relative performance under one-hop context does not establish that the shortcut is removed (\cref{tab:s-controls}). They do not change the downstream comparisons, which extract frozen vectors on unmasked graphs, but limit what can be inferred about the structure learned during pretraining.

More training windows improved reconstruction, but did not consistently improve downstream biological prediction (\cref{fig:s-scaling}). The effects differed by task and graph context. Reconstruction transfer, training-window scaling and exploratory masked-feature development are reported in the Supplementary Results. Those development experiments use a separate representation and do not replace the original $T$ in the comparisons above.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% Long Note: Following the examples, close by explaining what the result changes,
% then its limits and the next discriminating test. Established operators and
% unfinished candidates cannot be promoted into a new validated architecture.

Learning from a human pangenome requires a distinction between where a sequence lies and how it connects to alternative sequence arrangements. \method combines these two forms of context while retaining segment identifiers that link the graph to biological annotations. Freezing the encoder before biological evaluation separates representation learning from task-specific fitting. The methodological contribution lies in this use of native pangenome connections and genomic position; it does not require treating established attention or optimization operations as new algorithms.

The biological interpretation depends on the prediction target. Classifying a known structural variant asks how an ascertained event relates to graph structure; it does not test whether the model can discover that event or genotype it in a new individual. Likewise, identifying a regulatory element and predicting its measured activity are different questions. These distinctions are essential when assessing reuse across tasks. Graph complexity can describe the setting in which a prediction is made, but neither a complex region nor an association between complexity and a label establishes a biological mechanism.

A second distinction concerns the source of predictive information. Adding a graph representation to sequence features tests whether the representation is useful. Comparing trained and random-weight encoders with the same inputs measures the additional contribution of pretraining under those controls. A reconstruction objective must also be checked for cues introduced by the masking procedure itself. These controls are necessary before attributing biological performance to learned graph relationships or describing a representation as a broadly effective foundation model. The separate masked-feature development study addresses an alternative training objective and requires its own chromosome-level evaluation.

Several features of the study design limit the scope of inference. Holding out chromosomes does not hold out donors, and donors shared between graph resources complicate transfer between them. Sampling the ends of long segments restricts the context available to the sequence baseline. Aggregate graph segments lack donor paths and assign the same vector to both alleles at an SNV, preventing a direct interpretation of individual allelic effects. Insertion endpoint conventions and SV ascertainment further constrain the target population. Stronger sequence baselines, verified correspondence between segments and haplotypes, and donor-independent graphs are needed to address these limitations. Demonstrating improved genotyping would additionally require genotype truth, a defined set of callable loci and explicit evaluation in complex and copy-number-variable regions.

Further model development should therefore be judged by biological prediction beyond matched random encoders, graph statistics and sequence baselines. Comparisons must control the inputs and model capacity and reserve evaluation chromosomes from development decisions. This provides a testable criterion for improving pangenome representations: learning should improve held-out biological prediction beyond fixed graph summaries and sequence features.

\subsection{Oriented-segment representation and coupled coordinate--graph encoder}
\label{sec:architecture}
\label{sec:method-result}

The model operates on oriented segments and their native connections, rather than a linearized nucleotide input (\cref{fig:method}a,b). A segment $s$ has two handles, $2s+o$ for orientation $o\in\{0,1\}$. Reversing a handle toggles its orientation; the reverse-complement-equivalent relation to $(u,v)$ is $(v\mathbin{\mathrm{xor}}1,u\mathbin{\mathrm{xor}}1)$. The later canonical implementation uses this equivalence for candidate identity and masking. Historical training used exact directed rows, as detailed below; neither formulation makes the ordered pair scorer inherently reverse-complement equivariant.

Original v1 input $x_v$ has seven columns: genomic offset SO, segment length LN, rGFA SR annotation, GRCh38 membership, oriented degree, orientation and component index. SO, LN and degree are transformed by $\log(1+|x|)$ and divided by the maximum transformed value within the slice; SR and component index are min--max scaled, with constant columns set to zero. Membership and orientation remain binary. The structural columns are computed before query masking in original v1, a limitation addressed explicitly in the shortcut audit. No nucleotide embeddings or biological labels enter this encoder.

A shared input projection produces $h_v^{(0)}$. At layer $\ell$, coordinate attention $A_\ell$ and incoming-edge graph attention $G_\ell$ operate on the same evolving state. Thus ``coordinate'' specifies the attention neighborhood, not a branch restricted to coordinate-only inputs. With concatenation $\Vert$ and elementwise product $\odot$, the update is
\begin{align}
a_v^{(\ell)}&=A_\ell(h^{(\ell)},\mathrm{SO},o)_v, &
b_v^{(\ell)}&=G_\ell(h^{(\ell)},E_{\mathrm{visible}})_v,\\
g_v^{(\ell)}&=\sigma\!\left(W_\ell[a_v^{(\ell)}\Vert b_v^{(\ell)}]+c_\ell\right),\\
h_v^{(\ell+1)}&=\operatorname{LN}_\ell\!\left(h_v^{(\ell)}+g_v^{(\ell)}\odot a_v^{(\ell)}+(1-g_v^{(\ell)})\odot b_v^{(\ell)}\right).
\end{align}
The gate is learned per layer and feature; it is not only a final mixture of two independent embeddings. The principal backbone uses dimension 48, two layers and four heads. Graph attention follows the stored edge direction \citep{velickovic2018gat}; edge attributes were disabled in the principal configuration. Coordinate attention uses a window of SO-sorted node ranks, $k=\operatorname{clip}(\operatorname{int}[32(1+4b)],32,256)$, where $b$ is the slice branching fraction; this is not a base-pair radius. It combines three-scale rotary position encoding with orientation information \citep{su2021roformer}. The original sparse implementation normalizes attention over edges arriving at each destination and aggregates source values. We retain these original semantics when describing $T$; later bidirectional or chunked implementations are separate configurations. This combination adapts established attention and gating operations to native pangenome segments.

\subsection{Query masking, connection scoring and optimization}
\label{sec:objective}

The self-supervised target is observed connectivity within non-overlapping 5-Mb graph regions (\cref{fig:method}a,c). Positive candidates are observed adjacencies. Negative pairs are sampled within the same region, using the canonical benchmark's genomic-distance and unmasked-degree matching. They are not experimentally verified haplotype absences. Later canonical audits excluded conflicting reverse-complement-equivalent candidates; these post hoc exclusions do not retroactively change historical pretraining.

Historical v1 removed exact directed positive query rows for each candidate minibatch, together with candidate-level validation/test positives. Other training connections remained context. The later canonical implementation also removes reverse-complement-equivalent traversals. We audited the released graph link tables for equivalent rows, while retaining the historical masking convention when describing the original checkpoints. The saved dense prediction IDs do not allow all candidate identities to be reconstructed; audit findings and this limitation are reported in the \hyperref[sec:s-shortcut-controls]{Supplementary Results}. Masking uses one encoder pass per query set, not per pair or per entire set of training edges. Original degree/component columns remain unmasked, so distance matching does not require positive and negative candidates to have the same visible-degree deficit.

The ordered scorer consumes $[h_u\Vert h_v]$ through widths $2d\rightarrow2d\rightarrow d\rightarrow1$, layer normalization, ELU and dropout. Let $p=\sigma(s_{uv})$, $p_t=yp+(1-y)(1-p)$, $\alpha_t=0.25y+0.75(1-y)$, and $w_t=y\,n_-/\max(n_+,1)+(1-y)$ for candidate-batch counts $n_+,n_-$. The adopted binary focal objective \citep{lin2017focal} is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{edge}}=\operatorname{mean}_{(u,v)}[-\alpha_t w_t(1-p_t)^2\log p_t].
\end{equation}
Probabilities are clipped to $[10^{-7},1-10^{-7}]$. This pretraining scorer differs from the downstream SV pair features.

The principal launcher uses AdamW \citep{loshchilov2019adamw}, learning rate $5\times10^{-4}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$, dropout 0.1, DropEdge 0.1, 512 candidates per minibatch, four-batch gradient accumulation and gradient clipping at norm 1. Training is limited to 100 epochs with patience 20 and a mean per-window validation AUROC improvement tolerance of $10^{-4}$. The scheduler applies a five-step linear ramp followed by cosine decay and is stepped after each epoch: the first epoch uses the initial learning rate, followed by the recorded ramp. We state this ordering to reproduce the implemented schedule rather than implying a conventional warmup from the first update. These loss and optimizer choices are adopted techniques, not claimed methodological inventions.

\subsection{Frozen segment pooling and conditional information comparisons}
\label{sec:frozen-reuse}

After pretraining, eligible unmasked windows are encoded with fixed parameters (\cref{fig:method}d). The original segment cache averages embeddings over its oriented-handle occurrences across eligible windows. Biological examples join these vectors to the same interval/segment mapping used by their baseline features. Neither biological labels nor target-resource evaluation update the graph encoder or the independently pretrained Nucleotide Transformer.

Let $C$ denote the inherited coordinate/structural baseline, $K$ k-mer composition, $S$ frozen Nucleotide Transformer features and $T$ the original fused topology-pretrained features. Their definitions and joins are fixed across paired probes. We measure
\begin{align}
\Delta_{T\mid C,S}&=\operatorname{AUPRC}(C+S+T)-\operatorname{AUPRC}(C+S),\\
\Delta_{S\mid C,T}&=\operatorname{AUPRC}(C+S+T)-\operatorname{AUPRC}(C+T).
\end{align}
\label{sec:primary}
The first contrast tests additional graph-derived information, not a pretraining benefit by itself. The $H$ and matched random-encoder controls below test learned-weight attribution. $E$ denotes a separate sequence-conditioned development representation and is never substituted for historical $T$ results.

\subsection{Pangenome resources and graph preparation}


We analyzed graph resources derived from HPRC and HGSVC \citep{hprc2023draft,hgsvc2021,hgsvc32025}. We generated graph tables from GFA and GBZ resources using the variation graph toolchain \citep{garrison2018variation,siren2022gbz}. For each oriented segment, we recorded length, genomic coordinate when available, and connections to other oriented segments. HPRC-centered analyses used GRCh38-style chromosome names.

The processed HPRC R2 graph contains 751,237 segments and 1,097,658 links. The 303,425 downstream sequence-cache rows and 479,477 benchmark sequence-cache rows are distinct coverage denominators, not whole-graph segment counts. Full-coverage analyses used non-overlapping 5-Mb graph regions. Candidate records identify oriented endpoints and observed connection labels; genomic distance and endpoint degrees govern negative matching. The ordered scorer receives endpoint embeddings, not the connection label.

Path-bearing resources were audited separately from the SV-resolution training graph (\cref{tab:s-path}). Donor and haplotype counts from those resources are not treated as reconstructed donor provenance for the training graph. The fixed graph and checkpoint versions were retained. The SV graph itself has no donor paths; full-resolution path node IDs cannot be substituted for its segments without a verified correspondence.

\subsection{Strict and one-hop graph context}


We evaluate two graph contexts. In the strict setting, message passing is restricted to the native 5-Mb region. In the one-hop setting, the encoder additionally receives segments directly connected to the region. Reconstruction removes queried connections according to the historical or canonical protocol described above; frozen downstream extraction uses unmasked graphs within the same regional boundaries. The settings use the same genomic-region partition and archived run labels, but expansion can change the eligible candidate set. Their candidate counts are not assumed identical. Because one-hop expansion exposes more nodes and connections, strict and one-hop are treated as separate prediction contexts rather than exposure-matched estimates of a causal neighborhood-radius effect.

\subsection{Chromosome-held-out evaluation}

We partitioned the primary chromosomes into five rotating folds. Fold A tested chr1, chr6, chr11, chr16, and chr21; fold B tested chr2, chr7, chr12, chr17, and chr22; fold C tested chr3, chr8, chr13, chr18, and chrX; fold D tested chr4, chr9, chr14, chr19, and chrY; and fold E tested chr5, chr10, chr15, and chr20. The next fold in the rotation served as validation, with fold E validated on fold A; all remaining chromosomes were used for training. Original v1 used three independent executions labeled 42, 314159 and 20260806. These labels controlled split/order generators but did not fully seed PyTorch initialization or DropEdge in the historical trainer. They identify archived executions rather than exactly reproducible initialization seeds. Later campaigns use the repaired global seeding implementation; original checkpoint results are not retroactively relabeled as deterministic reruns.

Chromosome holdout prevents the same local genomic region from appearing in both training and evaluation. It is not a donor holdout. At least five donors (HG00733, HG02818, NA19036, NA19240, and HG002/NA24385) occur in both HPRC and HGSVC, so cross-resource analyses measure transfer between graph resources rather than donor-independent generalization.

\subsection{Reconstruction and cross-resource transfer}

We evaluated within-resource reconstruction on HPRC R2, HGSVC3, and integrated graph regimes. The primary metric was area under the precision--recall curve (\auprc), reported together with candidate prevalence; random-ranking AUPRC equals prevalence. For cross-resource and cross-version transfer, a trained model was applied to a different evaluation graph without target-resource adaptation. Strict and one-hop contexts were evaluated separately.

\subsection{Baselines, component controls, and capacity}

We evaluated four baselines on the same segment pairs as \method. The coordinate baseline used genomic coordinates, the k-mer composition baseline used nucleotide composition, the degree-sum baseline used the sum of endpoint node degrees, and the preferential-attachment baseline used their product. Degree sum and preferential attachment test whether a learned representation improves on simple connectivity statistics associated with the queried endpoints.

Degrees were computed on each context's actual visible graph, including the expanded one-hop graph, after removing the positive query edge and its reverse-complement counterpart. A replay audit recomputed the baseline scores from these graphs and compared them with the archived values.

Native 5-Mb complexity is an equally weighted mean of six robustly normalized measurements: log-transformed connection density, branching fraction, log maximum degree, log cycle-rank density, log segment density, and log segment-length interquartile range divided by median length. Except for branching fraction, transformations use $\log(1+x)$. Components are centered by their median and scaled by $1.4826$ times median absolute deviation; zero-scale components contribute zero, and standardized values are clipped to $[-5,5]$. The fixed score boundaries are $-0.155641$ and $0.434725$, giving 203 low-, 202 medium-, and 203 high-complexity windows. Thresholds were defined without model outcomes and reused across contexts. SV strata use the maximum endpoint category, with a start-only sensitivity; single-locus tasks use their mapped coordinate.

Component analyses removed the coordinate branch, graph branch, adaptive genomic window, fusion gate, or positional encoding. A separate capacity analysis compared hidden-dimension/graph-layer combinations of 24/1, 96/4, and 192/6 under the chromosome-held-out design.
The principal 48/2 model was recovered from the archived main matrix for a matched capacity comparison with identical fold/execution cells and held-out candidate counts. The capacity analysis calculates AP after pooling held-out predictions within each fold, whereas the principal reconstruction analysis averages window-level AP. Because AP is nonlinear, these summaries are not interchangeable.

\subsection{Sequence embeddings}

We generated 512-dimensional sequence embeddings with the Nucleotide Transformer v2 50M multi-species model \citep{dalla2025nucleotide}. Model revision \texttt{81b29e5786726d891dbf929404ef20adca5b36f1} was used for all analyses, and its parameters remained frozen.

For sequences within the model input limit, we averaged the final hidden state over non-special, non-padding tokens (maximum 1,000 tokens). For longer segments, we sampled equal amounts of sequence from the beginning and end, up to 6,000 bases in total, with an N separator before applying the fixed tokenizer's maximum-length rule. Before merging processing batches, we checked embedding dimensions, numerical finiteness, segment-identifier uniqueness, model-version consistency and coverage of each task. Coverage and sampling outcomes are reported in the \hyperref[sec:sequence-coverage-result]{Results}.


\subsection{Cis-regulatory element classification}

We derived \ccre labels from the ENCODE registry and assigned them to HPRC graph segments \citep{encode2020registry}. Downstream classifiers were trained under the same rotating chromosome partitions using fixed combinations of coordinates, k-mer composition, sequence embeddings, and graph embeddings.
The original cCRE/INS--DEL probes used a training-fitted StandardScaler followed by balanced logistic regression (L2, $C=1$, lbfgs, maximum 800 iterations). The original architecture and regularization were fixed across those tasks; the completed extensions below disclose their separate 4,000-iteration budgets and probe sensitivities. Temperature calibration and the F1 operating threshold used validation chromosomes only. The mean per-run training size was 182,054 examples (range 164,434--201,721); validation and test each averaged 60,685 examples (50,846--73,988), rather than 60,685 combined.

\subsection{Structural-variant insertion/deletion classification}

We used HGSVC sequence-resolved INS/DEL records of at least 50 bp and mapped their GRCh38 endpoint coordinates to the existing HPRC R2 SV-resolution segments. The target is insertion ($y=1$) versus deletion ($y=0$), conditional on an existing variant record. It does not test breakpoint detection, inversion prediction, somatic status, pathogenicity or clinical effect. The mean training size was 104,343 examples (91,932--116,366); validation and test each averaged 34,781 (27,708--43,217).

For each information source, the ordered pair representation is $[x_{\rm left}\Vert x_{\rm right}\Vert|x_{\rm left}-x_{\rm right}|\Vert x_{\rm left}\odot x_{\rm right}]$. It is not invariant under swapping endpoints. When an insertion lacks an END field, the legacy normalizer infers a pseudo-end from variant/reference length; this is a representation convention rather than a second biological insertion breakpoint. We preserve that convention for manuscript comparability. Class-only results report insertion sensitivity or deletion specificity at the original validation-derived threshold; AP and AUROC are undefined within a single target class.

\subsection{EN-TEx biological transfer}

EN-TEx provides personal epigenomic measurements and supplied allele-specific (AS) calls \citep{rozowsky2023entex}. The AS-prone cCRE task (P0) aggregates informative measurements at each locus. A positive has at least one supplied significant call; a negative has accessible tested measurements and no significant call. Unmeasured elements are not negatives. Multiple overlapping graph segments are mean-pooled using the existing mapper; length-weighted pooling is supported. Assay, donor, tissue and measurement counts are retained. Before fitting, we specified exact 1:1 exposure matching by chromosome and informative-measurement count, plus separate H3K27ac-only and CTCF-only P0 tasks. This sensitivity controls measurement opportunities rather than read depth or all sources of ascertainment.

The enhancer-state task (P1) uses explicitly active or repressed distal enhancer-like loci, excluding conflicting locus/tissue states and assigning annotations from ENCODE V2 registry ENCFF924IMH. Tissues were ranked before fitting by the size of their smaller class, retaining the five largest eligible datasets and requiring at least 1,000 loci per class. Separate probes use identical chromosome folds; tissue macro estimates weight the five tissues equally within each fold/seed.

The allele-specific SNV task (P2) uses CTCF and H3K27ac records from the default accessible SNV call set, parsed in chunks. Each SNV uses its containing segment's frozen features. All occurrences of a genomic position remain on the same chromosome partition across donors and tissues. Measurement multiplicities are retained through count-equivalent probe fitting; all occurrences of a locus receive the same prediction. This tests locus-level susceptibility, not donor-specific imbalance direction. Read counts, p-values and supplied AS calls never enter input features. The RNA ASE and ATAC/H3K4me3/H3K27me3 extensions use the same chromosome-partitioned locus-feature framework. Data coverage and paired comparisons, including every predefined P0 sensitivity, are reported in the \hyperref[sec:s-entex]{Supplementary Results} and \cref{tab:s-entex}.

\subsection{Structural strata and donor analysis}

All strata reuse archived test predictions with identical feature universes. Native complexity, inherited size/frequency bins, carrier count and class were specified before the new subgroup scores. We report within-stratum AP, AUROC and prevalence-normalized AP, $(\mathrm{AP}-\pi)/(1-\pi)$, where $\pi$ is positive prevalence. This rescaling is not fully prevalence invariant. Strata require at least 100 examples and 20 of each class in every run for the adequately powered designation. Sparse and undefined outcomes remain in the tables.

Phased carrier assignments were joined by variant ID and coordinate from the original 65-donor VCF. Missing alleles remain unknown. Verified sample aliases identify HG002 and NA24385 as the same donor \citep{ncbi_hg002}; the five overlapping donors are marked separately. Donor and haplotype scores stratify the existing pooled, chromosome-held-out probes. They do not constitute donor-held-out fitting or personal graph embeddings. Because donors share variants, uncertainty is summarized over chromosome folds and seeds rather than independent donor resampling.

\subsection{Statistical analysis}

All method comparisons were paired within biological task, chromosome fold, random seed, graph context, and information-source combination. We estimated confidence intervals with 10,000 hierarchical bootstrap replicates, resampling chromosome folds as the outer unit and random seeds within folds. This procedure preserves dependence among methods evaluated on the same held-out examples. Context interactions were defined as the one-hop contribution minus the strict-context contribution. Confidence intervals are reported for individual comparisons and are not adjusted for multiplicity across the full experiment suite. For historical SV subgroup analyses, exact two-sided sign flips use seed-averaged fold gains, and Benjamini--Hochberg adjustment is applied within each context, stratum family and metric. The completed SV-type studies adjust within each metric over all declared classes/macro summaries, contexts and contrasts. The H/random analysis likewise reports within-metric BH results. No chromosome confidence interval is assigned to the one-block masked-feature development study. Five nonzero fold differences give a minimum two-sided p-value of 0.0625; the pending four-fold primary summary has a minimum of 0.125. Positive bootstrap intervals do not override this limited exact-test resolution or establish BH-confirmed significance. Overlapping cross-validation training sets and correlated loci limit causal or population-level interpretation. Reconstruction intervals use 5,000 chromosome-block bootstrap draws retaining window counts. The reconstruction headline averages window-level AP, whereas the capacity diagnostic averages AP after pooling predictions within each fold; the statistics are labeled separately.

\subsection{Supplementary analyses and reproducibility}

We extracted haplotype paths from GBZ graph files and linked them to full-resolution segment indexes. Observed traversals were sampled within 5-Mb windows. We did not generate path-specific unobserved examples because absence from an aggregate graph and absence from a single haplotype require different definitions.

For external-signal overlap, we selected the highest-scoring 10\% of 5-Mb regions within each chromosome and matched each to five controls on region length, GC content, Umap/Bismap mappability \citep{karimzadeh2018umap}, graph complexity, HPRC variant density, and distance to a GENCODE v50 gene \citep{frankish2023gencode}. Signal sets included chr8 eQTL and sQTL positions \citep{lappalainen2013transcriptome}, all-trait GWAS Catalog positions, and Parkinson-filtered positions \citep{sollis2023gwas}.
The QTL analysis was a prespecified chromosome-8 pilot: only the chr8 HGSVC2 summary files were retrieved after checking download size and local storage; this restriction was not selected from enrichment outcomes.
We used 10,000 one-sided matched permutations and 2,000 bootstrap replicates for interval estimation.

For each principal analysis, we recorded model settings, trained-model version, held-out chromosomes, random seed, graph context, excluded conflicting pairs, predictions, and performance metrics. Summaries required all prescribed fold-seed combinations. Exact software versions and file-integrity records are retained in the accompanying provenance package.

\subsection{Separating neighborhood information from simple connectivity cues}
\label{sec:s-shortcut-controls}

In the original one-hop benchmark, the relative performance reversed. Compared with preferential attachment, \method had mean \auprc differences of $-0.0370$, $-0.0320$, and $-0.0420$ in low-, medium-, and high-complexity regions under strict context, with all intervals excluding zero. The similarity of the observed differences across complexity strata does not support a monotonic increase in \method advantage with graph complexity; the differences vary more between the evaluated contexts than across complexity strata, without isolating a neighborhood-radius effect.



The link-table audit found no duplicate reverse-complement-equivalent link rows in the released main graphs. However, historical dense prediction identifiers do not establish the absence of equivalent candidates, leaving that possible source of leakage unresolved. A separate replay reproduced 271,796 baseline scores over 1,215 context-specific slices exactly, with a maximum absolute difference of zero.

The strict-context baselines demonstrate that hidden-edge prediction can be highly predictable from endpoint degree alone. The completed visible-graph audit reproduced both contexts exactly, while the contrast still does not by itself establish a biological mechanism. Component controls show that graph information contributes substantially, whereas several fusion/position components do not provide large independent gains in the current runs. \subsection{Coverage of sequence embeddings}
\label{sec:s-coverage}

We generated 512-dimensional Nucleotide Transformer embeddings for 303,425 unique graph segments, covering every segment required for the \ccre and SV tasks. All four processing batches used the same archived model, and quality-control checks confirmed finite values and unique segment identifiers. For segments exceeding the model input limit, we sampled equal sequence from the beginning and end, up to 6,000 bases before applying the standard maximum-length rule. This procedure affected 26.3\% of required segments and motivates the scoped interpretation of the reciprocal sequence contribution in the main text.

\subsection{Graph complexity and donor-stratified SV effects}


The new complexity analysis reused all 30 archived probe runs. Strict-context topology gains were 0.0419 in low-, 0.0380 in medium-, and 0.0302 in high-complexity windows; one-hop gains were 0.0499, 0.0474 and 0.0336, respectively (\cref{fig:sv-complexity}). Thus these data do not support the hypothesis that the representation helps more strongly in high-complexity SV regions. Exact five-fold sign-flip tests gave interaction $p=0.125$ and $p=0.0625$; the pointwise bootstrap intervals should not be read as multiplicity-adjusted mechanistic evidence.

\subsection{EN-TEx data coverage and biological transfer}
\label{sec:s-entex}

The AS-prone cCRE dataset contained 5,330,335 informative measurements at 250,722 testable loci: 28,092 positive (11.20\%) and 222,630 negative. ENCODE V2 registry ENCFF924IMH matched all active/repressed source accessions, whereas later registry versions left unmatched accessions. The five tissues meeting the predefined count-based selection rule were thyroid gland, tibial nerve, pancreas, gastroesophageal sphincter and Peyer's patch.

The supplied high-confidence SNV file contained only RNA-seq records. Parsing the 22,310,439 rows in the default accessible call set retained 2,391,015 CTCF measurements at 596,650 loci (3.51\% AS) and 3,168,525 H3K27ac measurements at 713,418 loci (2.36\% AS). These assay-specific datasets supported the planned functional comparisons.

EN-TEx provided a broader test of regulatory activity \citepS{supp__rozowsky2023entex}. Its 450 completed fold/seed/context runs cover primary tasks, exposure-matched and assay-specific sensitivities, five tissues and additional assays; they are not independent biological replications. AS-prone cCRE prediction was inconclusive, including a strict gain of 0.00021 ($-0.00032$ to 0.00075). Active-versus-repressed distal enhancer tissue-macro gains were also inconclusive: 0.00089 ($-0.00039$ to 0.00233) strict and 0.00080 ($-0.00153$ to 0.00247) one-hop. Exposure matching changes ascertainment and prevalence, so absolute AP across matched and primary populations does not measure model improvement.

CTCF allele-specific SNV gains were 0.00147 (0.00010--0.00355) strict and 0.00225 (0.00096--0.00348) one-hop. H3K27ac was inconclusive under strict context, 0.00069 ($-0.00057$ to 0.00192), and positive under one-hop context, 0.00334 (0.00012--0.00796). RNA ASE, ATAC and H3K27me3 intervals included zero in both contexts. H3K4me3 gains had positive pointwise intervals, with estimates 0.00442/0.00900. \subsection{Controlled training-window scaling}

We retained the same 52,033-parameter encoder and connection scorer, exact HPRC R2 graph, objective, five chromosome folds and three seeds. Training windows were selected by a deterministic chromosome-balanced ranking at requested fractions of 12.5\%, 25\%, 50\% and 100\%; smaller sets are nested. Rounding within each chromosome and inherited unusable-slice exclusions mean realized fractions differ slightly from these requested values. Validation and test windows were unchanged, and the saved validation/test candidate identities and labels match exactly across fractions within every fold, seed and context. All 120 runs completed with the same 100-epoch limit and patience of 20. Parameter shapes, checkpoint metadata and source hashes were audited. The 100\% comparator was newly trained with the same current deterministic seeding and canonical-conflict exclusion, rather than substituted from historical results.

Pooled held-out-fold AP increased from 0.9184 at 12.5\% to 0.9292, 0.9375 and 0.9420 at 25\%, 50\% and 100\% under strict context. One-hop values were 0.9635, 0.9721, 0.9847 and 0.9900. The paired 100\%-minus-12.5\% gains were 0.02360 (95\% CI 0.02155--0.02575) and 0.02646 (0.02529--0.02792), respectively. Intervals use the paired fold/seed bootstrap. These scores pool candidates within each held-out fold and therefore differ from the window-mean reconstruction headline. Mean completed epochs ranged from 77.5 to 99.7 across context/fraction groups; training time and optimizer updates were not held constant. This establishes a reconstruction learning curve under the stated training protocol, not a scaling law, a diversity-scaling result, or demonstrated improvement in biological transfer.

The complete 120-run window-scaling experiment shows increasing held-out reconstruction as more training windows are available (\cref{fig:s-scaling}). This is a data-and-compute learning curve: the architecture and epoch limit are fixed, but optimizer updates are not held constant. The completed 360-run frozen biological evaluation is task dependent: the 100\%-minus-12.5\% C+S+T AP change was positive under strict SV transfer (+0.00348, 95\% CI 0.00309--0.00385) but negative under one-hop SV transfer (-0.00151, 95\% CI -0.00283-- -0.00066); cCRE improved in one-hop context (+0.00090, 0.00037--0.00146) but was inconclusive in strict context (-0.00032, -0.00066--0.00003), and CTCF intervals included zero. Thus intrinsic reconstruction scaling does not imply universal biological transfer.
