\begin{abstract}
Human genome analysis depends on how genetic diversity is represented. Pangenome graphs encode alternative sequence arrangements, but most genomic foundation models learn from linear DNA. We developed \method, a self-supervised model that couples genomic-position attention with attention over graph connections. We froze its representations for biological prediction. Across held-out chromosomes, graph features increased average precision beyond coordinates and nucleotide embeddings by 0.003--0.004 for regulatory-element classification and 0.034--0.040 for insertion-versus-deletion classification. Reconstruction transferred more effectively across graph resources with immediate-neighbourhood context, although masking cues and shared donors limit interpretation. Graph statistics and untrained encoders recovered much of the biological gain, and connection counts exposed a shortcut in pretraining. Our results support pangenome connectivity as a complementary genomic representation, while showing that the contribution of learned weights must be established separately.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Human genome analysis relies on a reference sequence to locate genes, regulatory elements and variants. GRCh38 and the telomere-to-telomere CHM13 assembly provide widely used coordinate systems\cite{schneider2017grch38,nurk2022complete}, but neither directly represents the alternative sequence arrangements found across individuals. Sequences absent from a reference must be described relative to it, complicating alignment, variant discovery and annotation in structurally diverse regions\cite{paten2017genome,eizenga2020pangenome}. Pangenome graphs represent these alternatives as connected segments, with different genomic arrangements following different routes through the graph\cite{garrison2018variation,li2020minigraph,hickey2024pangenome}. Haplotype-resolved assemblies from the Human Pangenome Reference Consortium (HPRC) and Human Genome Structural Variation Consortium (HGSVC) provide the basis for studying human genomes in this form\cite{hprc2023draft,hgsvc2021,hgsvc32025}.

The choice of genome representation affects which biological signals an analysis can recover. Assembly-based pangenomes improve access to structurally diverse loci and support variant discovery\cite{hprc2023draft,hgsvc32025}, while pangenome-based inference enables known structural variants (SVs) to be genotyped across large cohorts\cite{siren2021giraffe,ebler2022pangenie}. In functional genomics, Macias-Velasco and colleagues compared reference, personal and pangenome representations across accessibility, expression, methylation and chromatin-contact assays; the effects of genome choice depended on the assay and its analysis pipeline\cite{macias2026benchmarking}. The structural context represented by a pangenome may also be useful as an input to predictive models. Whether it contributes information beyond nucleotide sequence and genomic position remains a question for representation learning.

Genomic foundation models provide a natural sequence baseline for this question. DNABERT, DNABERT-2, HyenaDNA and the Nucleotide Transformer learn reusable features from DNA and support regulatory and variant-related prediction\cite{ji2021dnabert,zhou2024dnabert2,nguyen2023hyenadna,dalla2025nucleotide}. Related approaches score variants from masked-nucleotide likelihoods\cite{benegas2023gpn} or predict regulatory activity directly from sequence\cite{avsec2021enformer}. Although their training data may contain population diversity, each input is a linear string without an explicit description of how alternative segments connect. Frozen sequence representations also vary in their utility across tasks and do not consistently outperform simpler regulatory baselines\cite{tang2025evaluating}. Assessing a graph representation therefore requires a comparison with a specified sequence model on the same biological examples.

Pangenome topology describes relationships that genomic coordinates and nucleotide composition do not specify. It records where alternative routes branch and rejoin, and the orientation of the intervening segments. Two segments with similar sequences can therefore occupy different structural contexts. Genomic proximity and graph adjacency also need not coincide: alternative alleles may occupy similar reference positions without being directly connected, whereas the flanks of a deletion can be far apart in reference coordinates but adjacent in the graph. Learning from a pangenome requires a representation of both relationships. Aggregate connectivity describes the alternatives present at a locus; identifying the route followed by an individual additionally requires verified haplotype paths.

Pangenome information has entered predictive models through several representations (Fig.~\ref{fig:paradigms}). DeepGene converts graph-derived information into sequence input\cite{zhang2025deepgene}, whereas PangenomeX uses a phylogeny-guided network of copy-number relationships among individuals for supervised prediction\cite{xue2025pangenomex}. Self-supervision on native segment connectivity offers another route. Graph attention\cite{velickovic2018gat} and reconstruction of connections or masked node features\cite{kipf2016vgae,hu2020strategies,hou2022graphmae} provide established building blocks for this task. In link prediction, positive and negative sampling can introduce cues that inflate performance\cite{li2023heart,dong2022fakeedge}, a form of shortcut learning\cite{geirhos2020shortcut}. Evaluating pangenome pretraining consequently requires controls for the information already available in graph statistics and in an untrained encoder.

Here we introduce \method, a self-supervised model that learns segment representations directly from human pangenome connectivity (Fig.~\ref{fig:method}). Its encoder retains segment orientation and couples attention over nearby genomic positions with attention over connected segments through a feature-wise gate. We pretrain the model to recover hidden connections, then freeze it for biological prediction. On held-out chromosomes, its representations add a small contribution to candidate cis-regulatory element (cCRE) classification and a larger contribution to distinguishing known insertions from deletions, beyond genomic features and frozen nucleotide embeddings. EN-TEx allele-specific activity\cite{rozowsky2023entex} and external datasets test the limits of this reuse. Graph statistics and untrained encoders recover much of the predictive gain, while reconstruction controls expose a cue introduced by masking. These comparisons distinguish the value of graph context from the contribution of learned weights and motivate alternative pretraining objectives.

\subsection{Graph context improves structural-variant classification beyond sequence}
\label{sec:sv-result}

We evaluated frozen PangenomeFM representations ($T$) alongside genomic coordinate and structural features ($C$) and frozen Nucleotide Transformer embeddings ($S$). Performance is measured by average precision (AP), which summarizes the precision--recall trade-off. Strict context restricts the graph to each 5-Mb region; one-hop context includes directly connected segments. Biological labels are used only to fit the downstream classifier.

We first asked whether graph connectivity could help distinguish known SV types beyond genomic position and nucleotide sequence. We classified HGSVC3 insertions and deletions of at least 50~bp\cite{hgsvc32025} using features from the graph segments at each event's two endpoints. Adding $T$ to $C+S$ increased AP from 0.8721 to 0.9063 under strict context and to 0.9122 under one-hop context, with paired gains of 0.0342 (\CI 0.0324--0.0362) and 0.0401 (0.0361--0.0440), respectively (Fig.~\ref{fig:sv}a,b). One-hop context added 0.0058 (0.0033--0.0084) beyond strict context.

Sequence embeddings improved on k-mer composition by 0.0808 (Supplementary Table~\ref{S-stab:factorial}), but added little once graph representations were included: adding $S$ to $C+T$ changed AP by $-0.0002$ and $-0.0005$, with both intervals spanning zero. Thus, the additional contribution of this sequence representation depended on the baseline; the reciprocal comparison does not establish that graph context captures all sequence information relevant to SVs.

The topology gain was positive in all four allele-frequency groups and in every adequately powered length group. It was largest for 50--100-bp events (0.0475 strict, 0.0547 one-hop; Fig.~\ref{fig:sv}c,d and Supplementary Table~\ref{S-stab:svlength}). Events of 100~kb--1~Mb had negative, highly uncertain estimates, with about 27 test examples per run.

We then examined whether graph complexity explained the variation in topology gain. Using a score fixed before model evaluation (Methods), we found that strict gains decreased from 0.0419 to 0.0380 and 0.0302 across low-, medium- and high-complexity regions; one-hop gains were 0.0499, 0.0474 and 0.0336 (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{S-sfig:complexity}). High-minus-low differences were $-0.0117$ ($-0.0230$ to $-0.0019$) and $-0.0162$ ($-0.0267$ to $-0.0092$), although exact fold-level tests did not resolve these differences ($P=0.125$ and $P=0.0625$).

To assess overlap between the source populations, we stratified test events by carrier and excluded the five known donors shared between HGSVC3 and HPRC. Across the remaining 60 donors, mean gains were 0.0367 (0.0339--0.0395) strict and 0.0430 (0.0393--0.0470) one-hop (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{S-sfig:donor}). Variants from these donors on other chromosomes remained in the training set, so the analysis addresses resource overlap rather than prediction in unseen individuals.

\subsubsection{Classification across three structural-variant types}
\label{sec:sv-type-extension}

We extended SV classification to inversions to assess whether the graph contribution persisted across event types and after adjustment for event length. The analysis retained all 174,267 primary-chromosome events at their observed frequencies: 110,623 insertions, 63,346 deletions and 298 inversions. Each event was represented by the segment containing its first affected base. Adding $T$ to $C+S$ increased macro AP, the unweighted mean of the three one-versus-rest APs, from 0.4070 to 0.4740 under strict context and to 0.4586 under one-hop context (Table~\ref{tab:sv-natural}). After including event log length ($L$) and 14 label-free graph statistics ($H$), the increments from $T$ were 0.0477 (0.0399--0.0553) and 0.0150 (0.0077--0.0214).

The inversion-specific increments after $L$ and $H$ were uncertain, with intervals spanning zero in both contexts. The class contained only 298 events. Matching chromosome and length distributions across classes also made the macro gain after $H$ inconclusive (223 events per class; Supplementary Note~\ref{S-snote:threeclass}). For the natural-frequency macro contrasts, exact fold-level sign-flip tests reached the minimum attainable value of $P=0.0625$ (Benjamini--Hochberg $q=0.0741$ within the metric); the positive bootstrap intervals are therefore pointwise evidence. These analyses describe the ascertained HGSVC3 call set. Without an untrained-encoder comparison, we cannot determine how much of the macro gain comes from pretraining.

\subsection{Graph and sequence representations are complementary for regulatory elements}
\label{sec:ccre-result}

We next asked whether the same frozen graph representations could inform regulatory annotation. For ENCODE cCRE classification\cite{encode2020registry}, $C+S$ reached AP 0.9185. Adding $T$ increased AP to 0.9225 under strict context and 0.9219 under one-hop context, with paired gains of 0.0040 (0.0029--0.0048) and 0.0034 (0.0017--0.0048) (Fig.~\ref{fig:ccre}a,b). Sequence made a larger contribution than in SV classification: $S$ improved on k-mer composition by 0.3151 and added 0.0248 and 0.0261 beyond $C+T$ (Supplementary Table~\ref{S-stab:factorial}).

The graph contribution varied across regulatory annotations. Comparing each ENCODE class with the common background using the same binary classifier gave strict gains of 0.0058 for promoter-like elements, 0.0209 for proximal enhancer-like elements, 0.0054 for distal enhancer-like elements and 0.0349 for CTCF-only elements (Fig.~\ref{fig:ccre}c). Gains were larger in high-complexity regions (0.0076) than in low- and medium-complexity regions (0.0018 and 0.0014; Fig.~\ref{fig:ccre}d). These descriptive subgroup analyses reuse predictions from a single classifier.

EN-TEx provided a test of measured regulatory activity across tissues from four individuals\cite{rozowsky2023entex}. Because each segment has one representation, the allele-specific (AS) tasks predict a locus's susceptibility to imbalance across measurements. The contribution of $T$ was assay dependent (Supplementary Table~\ref{S-stab:entex}). AS-prone cCRE prediction was inconclusive in both contexts (strict gain 0.00021, $-0.00032$ to 0.00075), as was active-versus-repressed distal enhancer classification averaged over five tissues.

For AS single-nucleotide variants (SNVs), CTCF gains were 0.00147 (0.00010--0.00355) strict and 0.00225 (0.00096--0.00348) one-hop. H3K4me3 had positive intervals in both contexts and H3K27ac in one-hop context only; RNA allele-specific expression, ATAC-seq accessibility and H3K27me3 were inconclusive. Absolute AP was low (0.025--0.10 for SNV endpoints), consistent with the rarity of AS events.

We examined whether frequently measured loci accounted for these increments by giving each locus equal total weight at evaluation. CTCF intervals then included zero, whereas H3K27ac retained positive intervals in both contexts. H3K4me3 gains fell to approximately 0.0011 and were inconclusive; neither context passed the multiplicity sensitivity for the three-assay extension (Supplementary Note~\ref{S-snote:entex}). These comparisons show that the apparent benefit depends on which measurements receive weight. The predictions do not identify the favoured allele in an individual, and we interpret the positive findings as limited evidence for predicting a locus's propensity for imbalance.

\subsection{Sequence embeddings provide partial coverage of long segments}
\label{sec:sequence-coverage-result}

Sequence embeddings were available for all 303,425 segments used in cCRE and SV classification, with a 512-dimensional vector per segment and no missing features. However, 26.3\% of segments exceeded the Nucleotide Transformer's input limit and were represented by equal-length samples from their two ends, up to 6,000 bases in total. Complete segment coverage therefore did not imply complete sequence coverage. The small reciprocal contribution of $S$ to SV classification applies to this representation; a sequence baseline with access to whole segments could yield a different comparison. End sampling also excluded most tested variant bases in the external variant benchmarks described below. Extending the same embedding procedure to all 751,237 graph segments subsequently made sequence features available throughout the graph (Supplementary Note~\ref{S-snote:coverage}).

\subsection{Much of the graph contribution does not require pretraining}
\label{sec:controls-result}

To isolate the contribution of pretraining, we repeated the two binary tasks with graph statistics and untrained encoders (Table~\ref{tab:controls}). The graph-statistics baseline ($H$) comprises 14 label-free measurements from the complete graph. The untrained encoder ($R$) has the same architecture, inputs and representation-extraction procedure as \method. Together, these comparisons assess how much of the gain beyond $C+S$ is available before learning.

Adding $H$ to $C+S$ raised SV AP from 0.8721 to 0.9069. Beyond $C+S+H$, trained representations added 0.0154 (0.0140--0.0168) under strict context and 0.0109 (0.0085--0.0131) under one-hop context. The corresponding untrained increments were 0.0101 and 0.0105, accounting for about two-thirds of the strict increment and nearly all of the one-hop increment.

The trained-minus-untrained difference after $C+S+H$ was 0.0053 (0.0028--0.0105) for strict SV classification and close to zero for one-hop SV classification. For cCREs, it favoured the untrained encoder under strict context ($-0.0008$; $-0.0015$ to $-0.0001$) and was inconclusive under one-hop context. Without $H$, the trained encoder exceeded the untrained encoder by 0.0110 (0.0072--0.0201) for strict SVs but not in the other three settings. None of these contrasts survived multiplicity correction; with five folds, the smallest attainable exact sign-flip $P$ value is 0.0625.

Thus, graph representations contributed beyond sequence and the 14 statistics, but much of that contribution was retained without pretraining. The strict SV comparison provided the clearest pointwise evidence for an additional learned component. Such attribution depends on control representations as well as probe performance\cite{hewitt2019control}. We therefore examined whether the reconstruction objective could be solved using cues introduced by masking.

\subsection{Connection counts expose a shortcut in reconstruction pretraining}
\label{sec:shortcut-result}

On held-out chromosomes, connection-recovery AP was 0.9501 under strict context and 0.9880 under one-hop context, against a chance level of approximately 0.50 (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{S-sfig:controls}a and Supplementary Note~\ref{S-snote:reconstruction}). Under strict context, preferential attachment (the product of the endpoints' visible connection counts\cite{barabasi1999emergence,libennowell2007link}) reached 0.9835, exceeding \method. The sum of the connection counts reached 0.9742, compared with 0.7003 for genomic coordinates and 0.6555 for k-mer composition.

The masking procedure creates an asymmetry between positive and negative candidates. Hiding a true connection decreases the visible connection count at its endpoints, whereas a matched negative pair need not lose a connection. Complete-graph counts supplied to the encoder make this deficit potentially detectable. Recomputing the baselines on the visible graph reproduced the reported scores (Supplementary Note~\ref{S-snote:reconstruction}). Under one-hop context, \method exceeded preferential attachment in all complexity groups, but expansion changed both encoder inputs and baseline scores. This comparison alone cannot establish removal of the masking cue.

Removing graph attention reduced strict reconstruction AP to 0.7855. Increasing model capacity or the number of training regions improved reconstruction in some settings, without consistent gains in biological prediction (Supplementary Notes~\ref{S-snote:reconstruction} and~\ref{S-snote:scaling}). The 96-dimensional, four-layer model reached fold-pooled AP 0.9876, compared with 0.9423 for the principal model; these fold-pooled values differ from the region-averaged scores reported above.

Junction re-pairing removed the endpoint-exposure asymmetry by balancing positive and negative pairings and matching their signed genomic geometry. On the development partition, geometry and degree controls scored near chance (AP 0.500--0.507). Incoming-only message passing reached 0.501 versus 0.503 for an untrained encoder; bidirectional messages reached 0.790 versus 0.626. Sequence-conditioned variants reached 0.841 and 0.870 with different pair scorers (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{S-sfig:objectives}). These comparisons were used for design selection and lack independent chromosome replication.

The junction representation satisfied the biological development criterion in only one of three runs. A separate masked sequence-feature objective\cite{hou2022graphmae} produced positive trained-minus-untrained increments after $C+S+H$ for both tasks in all three development runs (Supplementary Note~\ref{S-snote:objectives}). Neither objective has a complete chromosome-level biological evaluation reported here.

\subsection{External evaluations show limited transfer}
\label{sec:generalization-result}

External evaluations gave limited evidence of transfer. For 69 clonal insertions and deletions in HG008, which contributed no training labels, adding $T$ to $C+S$ gave AP gains of 0.0325 ($-0.0534$ to 0.1103) strict and 0.0649 ($-0.0667$ to 0.2121) one-hop. Both intervals were wide and included zero. TraitGym\cite{benegas2025traitgym} contained 14,780 variants (11,400 complex-trait and 3,380 Mendelian), grouped as one putatively causal variant with nine controls. No topology contrast had a positive interval, either using locus-level features alone or after adding published allele scores (Supplementary Note~\ref{S-snote:external}). Each segment has a single vector regardless of the tested allele, and end sampling retained only 23--28\% of the variant bases in the sequence input; these constraints limit interpretation of the variant-level comparison.

At 265 complex loci with published COSIGT results\cite{bolognini2026cosigt}, we assessed whether graph representations could predict a per-locus summary of genotyping call quality. The training-median constant had lower mean absolute error (0.0462) than $C+S$ (0.0534) or $C+S+T$ (0.0527 strict, 0.0538 one-hop). Intervals for the reduction in error from adding $T$ spanned zero. This analysis concerns quality at evaluable loci; establishing an improvement in genotype calls would require genotype truth, a defined callable set and separate evaluation of complex regions. Reconstruction transfer across graph resources and releases is reported in Supplementary Note~\ref{S-snote:reconstruction}, with the limitations from masking cues and shared donors.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

We found that frozen pangenome graph representations complement nucleotide embeddings, with a larger contribution to insertion-versus-deletion classification than to regulatory-element annotation. This contribution persisted across chromosome partitions, but graph statistics and untrained encoders recovered much of it. After graph statistics were included, trained weights had their clearest pointwise advantage in strict SV classification; the comparisons did not survive multiplicity correction. The biological value of graph context is therefore better supported than a general benefit from pretraining. By coupling genomic proximity and graph adjacency, \method provides a way to study these contributions on the same segments and biological examples.

The expected role of graph context differs across biological tasks. SV type describes a change in sequence arrangement, making local connectivity directly relevant to the target. Regulatory annotation and measured activity depend on additional sequence and cellular features that an aggregate graph does not encode. In particular, a single vector shared by both alleles can describe a locus's structural setting but cannot identify the allele favoured in a donor or tissue. Extending this representation to individual function will require reliable links between segments, haplotype paths and matched functional measurements.

Our reconstruction analysis also identified a problem in the training task itself. Hiding a positive connection changes its endpoints' visible connection counts, whereas a matched negative pair need not undergo the same change. A model can exploit this asymmetry without learning the relationships that the task is intended to measure. Junction re-pairing removes the endpoint-exposure cue, and bidirectional models learned this revised task in development experiments. Masked sequence-feature reconstruction offers a second approach that leaves connectivity intact. Neither result yet establishes biological transfer across independent chromosome partitions, so reconstruction accuracy alone cannot determine which objective should be used.

The present evaluation also has limits imposed by the data and representations. Chromosome hold-out separates genomic regions but allows the same donors to contribute to training and testing; shared donors further complicate transfer between graph resources. Long-segment end sampling restricts the sequence baseline and warrants comparison with models that retain more nucleotide context. For SVs, ascertainment and the inferred second endpoint of insertions constrain interpretation to the event representations studied. Genotyping applications additionally require truth genotypes and a defined callable population, including complex and copy-number-variable regions.

Taken together, our results show where population graph structure contributes to biological prediction and where the current representation remains insufficient. Its stronger contribution to known SV types makes sequence arrangement a useful starting point for further development; the small, assay-dependent EN-TEx gains and inconclusive external tests argue against broad claims of functional transfer. Sequence-conditioned encoders may distinguish structurally similar segments by their nucleotide content, while path-aware representations could distinguish haplotypes traversing the same locus. We would evaluate both on chromosomes reserved from development, against matched untrained encoders, graph statistics and stronger sequence models. Such tests are needed to establish whether pretraining extracts biological information that these simpler representations leave unused.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Graph representation of pangenome segments}
\label{sec:representation}

\textbf{Graph resource.} We used the structural-variant-resolution graph of HPRC release~2, distributed in reference GFA (rGFA) format\cite{li2020minigraph}, in which each segment carries a stable source name (SN), an offset on that source (SO) and a source rank (SR; 0 for GRCh38). The graph contains 751,237 segments and 1,097,658 links. All segments carry SN, SO and SR tags; 305,572 are tagged as GRCh38 sequence. The graph stores no haplotype paths, so it does not directly identify the contributing genomes. Haplotype paths extracted from the full-resolution graphs of the same resources comprise 454 haplotypes from 227 individuals (HPRC release~2) and 122 haplotypes from 61 individuals (HGSVC3) (Supplementary Table~\ref{S-stab:paths}). % Long Note: confirm against the HPRC release documentation which assemblies were used to build the SV-resolution graph.
Graph tables were generated from GFA and GBZ files with the variation-graph toolkit\cite{garrison2018variation,siren2022gbz}. We partitioned primary chromosomes into non-overlapping 5-Mb regions and retained 608 regions for analysis.

\textbf{Oriented handles.} Each segment $s$ is represented by two handles, $2s+o$, where $o\in\{0,1\}$ denotes forward or reverse orientation. A directed link $(u,v)$ between handles and its reverse-complement traversal $(\bar v,\bar u)$, where $\bar u$ flips the orientation of $u$, describe the same junction read on opposite strands.

\textbf{Structural inputs.} Each handle $v$ receives a seven-dimensional input $x_v$: genomic offset (SO), segment length, source rank (SR), GRCh38 membership, number of connections (degree) at that handle, orientation and connected-component index. Offset, length and degree are transformed by $\log(1+|x|)$ and divided by the largest transformed value in the region; source rank and component index are min--max scaled within the region, with constant columns set to zero; membership and orientation are binary. Degree and component index are computed on the complete graph before any connection is hidden. No nucleotide sequence or biological label enters the encoder.

\textbf{Graph contexts.} In the strict context, message passing is restricted to the segments of a 5-Mb region. In the one-hop context, the encoder additionally receives segments directly connected to the region. Both contexts use the same regional partition. Expansion changes the visible segments and eligible candidates, so the comparison does not isolate neighbourhood radius.

\subsection{Coupled coordinate--graph encoder}
\label{sec:architecture}

A shared linear projection maps $x_v$ to $h_v^{(0)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$. At layer $\ell$, coordinate attention $A_\ell$ and graph attention $G_\ell$ act on the same state:
\begin{align}
a_v^{(\ell)}&=A_\ell\big(h^{(\ell)};\,\mathrm{SO},o\big)_v,\\
b_v^{(\ell)}&=G_\ell\big(h^{(\ell)};\,E_{\mathrm{visible}}\big)_v,\\
g_v^{(\ell)}&=\sigma\!\left(W_\ell\,[a_v^{(\ell)}\Vert b_v^{(\ell)}]+c_\ell\right),\\
h_v^{(\ell+1)}&=\operatorname{LayerNorm}_\ell\!\left(h_v^{(\ell)}+g_v^{(\ell)}\odot a_v^{(\ell)}+\big(1-g_v^{(\ell)}\big)\odot b_v^{(\ell)}\right),
\end{align}
where $\Vert$ denotes concatenation, $\odot$ the elementwise product and $\sigma$ the logistic function. Each layer learns a feature-wise gate $g_v^{(\ell)}$ from the two attention outputs. Their relative contributions can therefore vary across handles, layers and feature dimensions.

Coordinate attention operates over handles sorted by genomic offset. Each handle attends to $k=\operatorname{clip}\!\big(\lfloor 32(1+4b)\rfloor,32,256\big)$ neighbouring handles in this order, where $b$ is the regional branching fraction. Higher branching fractions give wider attention windows, measured in handle ranks rather than base pairs. Queries and keys receive rotary position encodings\cite{su2021roformer} of the genomic offset at three scales, together with an orientation encoding.

Graph attention follows graph attention networks\cite{velickovic2018gat}: attention weights are normalized over the connections arriving at each handle, and messages are aggregated from their source handles along the stored direction of each link. Edge attributes were not used. The principal model has $d=48$, two layers and four heads; together with the pair scorer it has 52,033 parameters.

\subsection{Masked connection pretraining}
\label{sec:objective}

\textbf{Candidates.} Within each region, positive candidates are observed links between handles. Negative candidates are pairs of handles in the same region that are not linked, sampled to match the positives in genomic distance and in complete-graph degree. Negatives describe absence from the aggregate graph, not experimentally verified absence from any haplotype. The positive fraction was 0.4990 in the strict context and 0.5065 in the one-hop context, so the chance level of AP is approximately 0.50.

\textbf{Masking.} Candidates were processed in minibatches of up to 512. Before each encoder pass, the positive connections queried in that minibatch were removed from the visible graph, together with all validation and test positives; the remaining training connections stayed visible as context. Queried connections were removed in the orientation in which they were queried. The released link tables contain no duplicated reverse-complement links, but candidate identities cannot all be recovered from the saved predictions. An equivalent traversal of a queried connection may therefore have remained visible for some candidates. This uncertainty qualifies reconstruction accuracy. Downstream representations were extracted from unmasked graphs, although their pretrained weights may reflect the original objective.

\textbf{Pair scorer and loss.} An ordered scorer maps $[h_u\Vert h_v]$ through layers of width $2d\rightarrow2d\rightarrow d\rightarrow1$ with layer normalization, ELU activations and dropout, giving a logit $s_{uv}$. With $p=\sigma(s_{uv})$ and label $y\in\{0,1\}$, let $p_t=yp+(1-y)(1-p)$, $\alpha_t=0.25y+0.75(1-y)$ and $w_t=y\,n_-/\max(n_+,1)+(1-y)$, where $n_+$ and $n_-$ are the numbers of positive and negative candidates in the minibatch. The training loss is the class-weighted focal loss\cite{lin2017focal}
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{edge}}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}|}\sum_{(u,v)\in\mathcal{B}}-\alpha_t\,w_t\,(1-p_t)^{2}\log p_t ,
\end{equation}
with probabilities clipped to $[10^{-7},1-10^{-7}]$. The scorer is not invariant to swapping $u$ and $v$, so candidate order is retained; this does not guarantee reverse-complement equivariance.

\textbf{Optimization.} We used AdamW\cite{loshchilov2019adamw} with learning rate $5\times10^{-4}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$, dropout 0.1, random removal of 10\% of visible connections during training (DropEdge), gradient accumulation over four minibatches and gradient clipping at norm 1. Training ran for at most 100 epochs, with early stopping after 20 epochs without an improvement of at least $10^{-4}$ in mean per-region validation AUROC. The learning rate followed a five-step linear ramp and cosine decay, updated once per epoch after the first epoch. Each configuration was trained three times. Run identifiers 42, 314159 and 20260806 fixed the data split and ordering. Weight initialization and DropEdge were not fully seeded, so these identifiers do not reproduce the initial model states exactly.

\subsection{Frozen representations and downstream comparisons}
\label{sec:frozen-reuse}

After pretraining, each region was encoded without masking and with fixed parameters. A segment's representation $T$ is the mean of its handle embeddings over all occurrences in eligible regions. Neither biological labels nor evaluation data update the graph encoder or the Nucleotide Transformer.

We compared fixed information sources on identical examples: $C$, genomic coordinates and basic structural descriptors of each example; $K$, k-mer composition; $S$, frozen Nucleotide Transformer embeddings; $T$, frozen \method representations; $H$, 14 label-free graph statistics; $R$, representations from an untrained encoder with the same architecture, inputs and extraction procedure as $T$; and, for the three-class task, $L$, measured event log length. $H$ was computed on the complete graph and therefore has access to a wider neighbourhood than the regional encoders; it is a practical structural baseline rather than a matched architectural ablation. The two primary contrasts are
\begin{align}
\Delta_{T\mid C,S}&=\mathrm{AP}(C+S+T)-\mathrm{AP}(C+S),\\
\Delta_{S\mid C,T}&=\mathrm{AP}(C+S+T)-\mathrm{AP}(C+T).
\end{align}
The first quantifies the incremental predictive value of $T$. To assess the contribution of pretraining, we additionally compare $C+S+H+T$ with $C+S+H$ and with $C+S+H+R$.

\subsection{Shortcut-resistant pretraining objectives}
\label{sec:objectives-methods}

\textbf{Junction re-pairing.} Connections at branching handles are ordered by genomic offset and grouped into spans of up to 16, and all connections in a span are hidden together, leaving a set of dangling outgoing and incoming ends. Negative candidates are cross-pairings of an outgoing end from one hidden connection with an incoming end from another in the same span, chosen by optimal assignment to match the genomic separation of the original connection. Only complete permutation cycles are retained, so every retained end occurs once in a positive and once in a negative pair and both share the same visible degree; connections that cannot be re-paired are excluded. Pairs are further required to fall in the same bins of signed genomic offset and signed gap, with bin edges growing by a factor of 1.25. Degree inputs are computed on the visible graph after masking.

We compared incoming-only and bidirectional message passing. The bidirectional variant adds a reverse message along each visible connection and learns a per-head attention bias for message direction. Each variant was paired with either the multilayer scorer or a linear interaction scorer; sequence-conditioned variants also received frozen Nucleotide Transformer embeddings. Untrained counterparts used matching architectures and run labels. Identical initial parameters were verified for the sequence-conditioned pairs; the earlier topology-only checkpoints lack the record needed to verify this equivalence. Models were developed under one-hop context on fold~A training chromosomes and evaluated on its validation chromosomes (2,050 candidate pairs in 104 regions). Training used at most ten epochs and early stopping after three.

\textbf{Masked sequence-feature reconstruction.} The sequence-conditioned encoder ($E$) uses the same backbone ($d=48$, two layers, four heads) with bidirectional messages, and receives the seven structural inputs together with the 512-dimensional Nucleotide Transformer embedding of each segment. For each training step, 30\% of segments are masked by replacing all 519 input columns at both of their handles with a learned token. The targets are the segments' Nucleotide Transformer embeddings, standardized with statistics from training segments only. A linear projection, zeroing of masked latent rows, one graph-attention decoder layer and a linear output reconstruct the targets under a scaled cosine error with exponent 2, adapting the masked graph autoencoder objective\cite{hou2022graphmae}. All connections remain visible because connections are not prediction targets.

For each of three runs, we compared four arms: full and coordinate-only encoders, each trained or left at its initial parameters. Training used at most 20 epochs and early stopping after five; the model state with the lowest self-supervised validation loss was retained. Frozen $E$ representations were evaluated with the same probe design as $T$, with a 4,000-iteration limit, on fold-A validation chromosomes. We required the trained-minus-untrained AP difference after $C+S+H$ to be positive for both tasks in every run before chromosome-level replication. That replication uses all five folds, with fold~B reported separately because its test chromosomes served as development validation.

\subsection{Chromosome-held-out evaluation}
\label{sec:folds}

Primary chromosomes were partitioned into five rotating folds (Supplementary Table~\ref{S-stab:folds}). Fold A tested chr1, chr6, chr11, chr16 and chr21; fold B chr2, chr7, chr12, chr17 and chr22; fold C chr3, chr8, chr13, chr18 and chrX; fold D chr4, chr9, chr14, chr19 and chrY; and fold E chr5, chr10, chr15 and chr20. The next fold in the rotation served as validation, with fold E validated on fold A, and all remaining chromosomes were used for training. The same partition governed pretraining and downstream classifiers. Chromosome hold-out prevents the same genomic region from appearing in training and evaluation, but it is not a donor hold-out; at least five donors (HG00733, HG02818, NA19036, NA19240 and HG002, also catalogued as NA24385\cite{ncbi_hg002}) occur in both HPRC and HGSVC.

\subsection{Sequence embeddings}
\label{sec:sequence-methods}

We generated 512-dimensional embeddings with the Nucleotide Transformer v2 50M multi-species model\cite{dalla2025nucleotide} (revision \texttt{81b29e5786726d891dbf929404ef20adca5b36f1}), with frozen parameters. Each segment's embedding is the mean of the final hidden states over non-special, non-padding tokens, with at most 1,000 tokens. Longer segments were represented by equal-length samples from their beginning and end, up to 6,000 bases in total, separated by an N before tokenization. Before merging processing batches, we checked embedding dimensions, finiteness, identifier uniqueness, model version and task coverage. Both orientations of a segment share its sequence embedding.

\subsection{Downstream tasks}
\label{sec:tasks}

\textbf{Probes.} Unless stated otherwise, each feature combination was standardized with training-set statistics and classified with class-balanced, L2-regularized logistic regression ($C=1$, L-BFGS, at most 800 iterations for the primary binary tasks and 4,000 for the extended analyses). Temperature calibration and the decision threshold used validation chromosomes only. Probe architecture and regularization were fixed across tasks and were not tuned on test chromosomes.

\textbf{Candidate cis-regulatory elements.} Labels were derived from the ENCODE registry\cite{encode2020registry} and assigned to HPRC graph segments. Per run, training sets averaged 182,054 examples (range 164,434--201,721), and validation and test sets each averaged 60,685 (50,846--73,988). Class-specific evaluations (PLS, pELS, dELS and CTCF-only) subset the predictions of the same binary classifier against the common background.

\textbf{Insertion versus deletion.} We used HGSVC3 sequence-resolved insertions and deletions of at least 50~bp\cite{hgsvc32025} and mapped their GRCh38 endpoint coordinates to graph segments. The target is event type among known variants: insertion ($y=1$) or deletion ($y=0$). Breakpoint discovery, pathogenicity and clinical effect were not evaluated. For each information source, an event is represented as $[x_{\mathrm{left}}\Vert x_{\mathrm{right}}\Vert |x_{\mathrm{left}}-x_{\mathrm{right}}|\Vert x_{\mathrm{left}}\odot x_{\mathrm{right}}]$. When an insertion lacked an END field, its second endpoint was inferred from the variant and reference lengths, a representation convention rather than a second biological breakpoint. Training sets averaged 104,343 events (91,932--116,366), and validation and test sets each averaged 34,781 (27,708--43,217).

\textbf{Three-class SV classification.} The primary-chromosome universe comprises 174,267 events (110,623 insertions, 63,346 deletions and 298 inversions); 2,264 events on non-primary contigs, including two inversions, were excluded. Each event is represented by the graph segment containing its first affected base, and events sharing an anchor segment were retained. We fitted separate balanced one-versus-rest probes for each class over 13 predefined feature combinations and report macro AP, the unweighted mean of the three class APs. The matched sensitivity analysis sampled equal class counts within fixed chromosome and length bins (223 events per class; 669 events).

\textbf{EN-TEx.} We used personal epigenomes and supplied allele-specific calls from EN-TEx\cite{rozowsky2023entex}. AS-prone cCREs were positive if any informative measurement had a significant AS call and negative if none did. Unmeasured elements were excluded. Before fitting, we specified an exposure-matched sensitivity (exact 1:1 matching on chromosome and number of informative measurements) and H3K27ac-only and CTCF-only versions.

The enhancer-state task classified distal enhancer-like elements as active or repressed, using ENCODE registry annotation ENCFF924IMH, in the five tissues ranked highest by the size of their smaller class, each with at least 1,000 elements per class (thyroid gland, tibial nerve, pancreas, gastroesophageal sphincter and Peyer's patch), averaged with equal weight.

The AS-SNV tasks used the default accessible call set; each SNV takes the representation of its containing segment, all occurrences of a position share a chromosome fold, and repeated measurements enter as weights. Read counts, $P$ values and AS calls never enter the features.

\textbf{External endpoints.} For HG008, prospectively refitted probes were evaluated on 69 mapped clonal insertions and deletions, with no HG008 label used for training.

TraitGym\cite{benegas2025traitgym} comprises 11,400 complex-trait and 3,380 Mendelian variants, each in a matched group with one positive and nine controls; we preserved groups and alleles and used our five chromosome folds in place of the official leave-one-chromosome-out protocol, with a sensitivity analysis adding published Nucleotide Transformer 2.5B allele scores ($V$).

For COSIGT\cite{bolognini2026cosigt}, we summarized 14,838 published sample--locus outcomes at 265 loci, treated 1,592 unsupported combinations as unmeasured, removed 24 exact duplicate records and used the mean quality-value fraction per locus as the primary continuous target, with ridge penalties chosen by validation mean absolute error and a training-median constant as reference.

\subsection{Reconstruction baselines, ablations and scaling}
\label{sec:baselines-methods}

Reconstruction was evaluated within HPRC release~2 and HGSVC3 and on integrated graphs, and for transfer between resources and releases without adaptation. Baselines were evaluated on the same candidate pairs as \method: genomic coordinates, k-mer composition, the sum of endpoint degrees and preferential attachment (their product). Degrees were computed on the graph visible in each context, including the expanded one-hop graph, after removing the queried connection and its reverse-complement counterpart; we recomputed all baseline scores from these graphs and compared them with the reported values.

Component ablations removed coordinate attention, graph attention, the adaptive window, the gate or positional encoding. The capacity analysis compared hidden-dimension and graph-layer settings of 24/1, 48/2, 96/4 and 192/6. For training-window scaling, we retained the architecture, objective, folds and runs and trained on nested, chromosome-balanced subsets containing 12.5\%, 25\%, 50\% and 100\% of training regions, with validation and test candidates unchanged.

\subsection{Graph complexity and stratified analyses}
\label{sec:complexity-methods}

Regional complexity is the equally weighted mean of six robustly standardized measurements: log connection density, branching fraction, log maximum degree, log cycle-rank density, log segment density and the log ratio of the interquartile range of segment length to its median. Except for branching fraction, transformations use $\log(1+x)$. Each component is centred on its median and scaled by 1.4826 times its median absolute deviation, with zero-scale components contributing zero and standardized values clipped to $[-5,5]$. The fixed boundaries $-0.155641$ and $0.434725$ define 203 low-, 202 medium- and 203 high-complexity regions. Thresholds were set without reference to model outcomes and reused across contexts and tasks; SV events take the higher category of their two endpoints.

Stratified analyses reuse the held-out predictions of the primary probes and report within-stratum AP, AUROC and prevalence-normalized AP, $(\mathrm{AP}-\pi)/(1-\pi)$. A stratum is considered adequately powered when every run contains at least 100 examples and 20 of each class.

For donor stratification, phased carrier assignments were joined from the HGSVC3 call set of 65 donors, with missing alleles left unknown; donor results stratify the pooled chromosome-held-out probes and do not constitute donor-held-out fitting.

\subsection{Statistical analysis}
\label{sec:stats}

All comparisons were paired within task, chromosome fold, pretraining run, graph context and feature combination. Confidence intervals were estimated from 10,000 hierarchical bootstrap replicates that resample folds and then runs within folds, which preserves the dependence between methods evaluated on the same examples. Context differences are defined as the one-hop contribution minus the strict contribution.

For attribution and subgroup analyses, exact two-sided sign-flip tests use the five run-averaged fold differences, so the smallest attainable $P$ value is 0.0625, and Benjamini--Hochberg adjustment is applied within each metric across the declared contrasts. Unless stated, intervals are pointwise and not adjusted across the full set of analyses.

Reconstruction intervals use 5,000 chromosome-block bootstrap draws that retain region counts. Reconstruction AP is averaged over regions, whereas the capacity and scaling analyses pool predictions within each fold; because AP is nonlinear these summaries are reported separately. Development experiments on a single fold are reported per run without confidence intervals.

\section{Graph resources and haplotype paths}
\label{S-snote:resources}

The primary graph is the structural-variant-resolution graph of HPRC release~2\cite{supp__hprc2023draft}. It contains 751,237 segments and 1,097,658 links, and every segment carries source-name, offset and source-rank tags; 305,572 segments are tagged as GRCh38 sequence. To examine the availability of haplotype information absent from this graph, we characterized the path-bearing full-resolution graphs of HPRC release~2 and HGSVC3\cite{supp__hgsvc32025} separately and assessed the correspondence between haplotype traversals and their segments (Supplementary Table~\ref{S-stab:paths}).

All selected HPRC and HGSVC paths mapped to segments of their full-resolution graphs, with no missing segment handles; linking these segments to the structural-variant-resolution graph requires a verified correspondence. Of 27,223 HGSVC path records, 13,680 had a primary-chromosome assignment and 13,543 could not be assigned. Sampled observed connections along each path were extracted within 5-Mb windows. We did not construct path-specific negative connections, because the absence of a connection from one haplotype does not imply its absence from the aggregate graph, and a valid haplotype-level negative requires a separate definition. These checks establish the availability of path traversals within the full-resolution graphs; they do not constitute a donor-held-out biological evaluation. Donor and haplotype counts from these resources describe the path-bearing graphs and are not a reconstruction of the donors contributing to the structural-variant-resolution graph.

Topology gains decreased with regional complexity. \section{Three-class SV classification}
\label{S-snote:threeclass}

\subsection{Data}
We re-derived event annotations from the source call set while retaining the original event identifiers, verifying insertion and deletion coordinates and allele lengths. The primary-chromosome set contains 110,623 insertions, 63,346 deletions and 298 inversions; 2,264 events on non-primary contigs, including two inversions, were excluded. Each event is anchored to the graph segment containing its first affected base. One terminal chrY segment used by 64 events lacked a representation in the original extraction. We generated its vectors with the same pretrained encoders, retaining every event and leaving the existing vectors unchanged. All probe fits in both the natural-frequency and matched analyses converged.

\subsection{Natural-frequency analysis}
Macro AP rose from 0.4070 for $C+S$ to 0.4740 strict and 0.4586 one-hop with $T$ (main Table~\ref{tab:sv-natural}). After $C+S+L+H$, the macro increments were 0.0477 and 0.0150, and the inversion-specific increments were 0.0152 ($-0.0043$ to 0.0365) strict and $-0.0015$ ($-0.0197$ to 0.0117) one-hop. Inversions had prevalence 0.0017, which sets the chance AP for that class.

\subsection{Matched sensitivity analysis}
To match chromosome and length-bin distributions between classes, we sampled 223 events of each type within fixed chromosome and length bins (669 events). This excludes 75 primary-chromosome inversions without matched support, whereas all 298 inversions remain in the natural-frequency analysis. In this matched population, macro AP was 0.4141 for $C+S$ and 0.4738 for $C+S+H$, higher than $C+S+T$ (0.4378 strict, 0.4409 one-hop), and adding $T$ after $H$ was inconclusive (Supplementary Table~\ref{S-stab:svmatched}). The matched and natural-frequency populations differ in prevalence and composition, limiting comparisons of their absolute AP. Neither three-class analysis includes an untrained-encoder arm.

\section{Sequence-embedding coverage}
\label{S-snote:coverage}

We generated 512-dimensional Nucleotide Transformer embeddings for all 303,425 segments required by the cCRE and SV tasks, in four processing batches with the same model revision. Quality checks confirmed finite values, unique segment identifiers and complete task coverage. Segments longer than the model's input limit (26.3\% of those required) were represented by equal samples from their two ends, up to 6,000 bases, before the tokenizer's maximum-length rule was applied. For these segments, $S$ summarizes the sampled ends and omits the intervening sequence. Reciprocal sequence contrasts therefore apply to this sampling procedure.

We subsequently extended the embeddings to all 751,237 segments of the graph with the same model and procedure. The 479,477 embeddings used by the reconstruction benchmark were preserved exactly, and 271,760 new embeddings were added, all finite. The extension provides sequence inputs across the graph. The analyses reported here, including Supplementary Note~\ref{S-snote:objectives}, used the original embeddings.

\section{EN-TEx datasets and complete results}
\label{S-snote:entex}

\subsection{Datasets}
The AS-prone cCRE dataset contains 5,330,335 informative measurements at 250,722 testable elements, of which 28,092 (11.20\%) are positive.

The ENCODE V2 registry annotation ENCFF924IMH matched all active and repressed source accessions, whereas later registry versions left accessions unmatched. The five tissues that met the predefined size rule for the enhancer-state task were thyroid gland, tibial nerve, pancreas, gastroesophageal sphincter and Peyer's patch.

The supplied high-confidence SNV file contained only RNA-seq records, so we parsed the 22,310,439 rows of the default accessible call set, retaining 2,391,015 CTCF measurements at 596,650 loci (3.51\% AS) and 3,168,525 H3K27ac measurements at 713,418 loci (2.36\% AS).

\subsection{Results}
All predefined endpoints are shown in Supplementary Table~\ref{S-stab:entex}. The exposure-matched version of the AS-prone cCRE task controls the number of measurement opportunities but targets a different population with higher prevalence, so its absolute AP cannot be compared with the primary task. AS-prone cCRE prevalence increases across complexity groups. Assessing predictive benefit requires comparison of $C+S$ and $C+S+T$ within each group, where both models face the same prevalence. Intervals are pointwise and unadjusted across the panel.


\subsection{Repeated-measurement and multiplicity sensitivities}
The same SNV can occur in several donor, tissue and assay measurements. We therefore re-evaluated saved predictions with each observation weighted by the inverse number of measurements at its locus. This changes the evaluation target; the probes were not refitted. With equal total weight per locus, CTCF gains were 0.00018 ($-0.00054$ to 0.00096) strict and 0.00049 ($-0.00034$ to 0.00132) one-hop. H3K27ac gains were 0.00199 (0.00111--0.00273) and 0.00221 (0.00065--0.00419), respectively. These two-assay follow-ups were specified after inspection of the primary results and are exploratory.

For the subsequent ATAC-seq, H3K4me3 and H3K27me3 extension, the follow-up protocol was fixed before fitting. Equal-locus H3K4me3 gains were 0.00107 ($-0.00005$ to 0.00243) strict and 0.00115 ($-0.00054$ to 0.00281) one-hop. In the multiplicity sensitivity, we averaged paired gains across runs within each fold, enumerated the 32 fold-level sign flips, and applied Benjamini--Hochberg correction to the six assay--context contrasts separately for each metric and weighting scheme. No AP contrast had $q<0.05$; H3K4me3 had $q=0.375$ with measurement weighting and $q=0.75$ with equal-locus weighting. Only five folds contribute to these tests, so the smallest attainable two-sided $P$ value is 0.0625. This analysis does not correct for every endpoint and stratum in the full study.

Equal-donor and equal-tissue macro evaluations use all groups with at least 200 measurements, ten positives and ten negatives in every held-out fold. They describe variation within the sampled donors and tissues, rather than transfer to unseen donors or tissues. Together, these sensitivities distinguish a small predictive increment from a broader claim about allele-specific biology.

\section{External evaluations}
\label{S-snote:external}

\subsection{HG008}
Prospectively refitted probes were evaluated on 69 mapped clonal insertions and deletions in HG008, with no HG008 label used for training. Gains beyond $C+S$ were 0.0325 ($-0.0534$ to 0.1103) strict and 0.0649 ($-0.0667$ to 0.2121) one-hop (Supplementary Table~\ref{S-stab:external}). The evaluation includes a single genome and only 69 events, limiting inference across individuals. Earlier probe-reproduction attempts were unsuccessful and are excluded from these estimates.

\subsection{TraitGym}
All 14,780 matched variants\cite{supp__benegas2025traitgym} were retained in our five-fold adaptation. Neither the frozen-feature study nor a sensitivity analysis that added published Nucleotide Transformer 2.5B allele scores ($V$) produced a positive topology interval. Before tokenization, the sequence input retained only 22.95\% of tested complex-trait bases and 27.75\% of Mendelian bases, which limits any locus-level sequence representation on this benchmark.

In the allele-score sensitivity, the published score alone reached Mendelian AP 0.1604, whereas $C+S+V$ reached 0.1021 compared with 0.1208 for $C+S$, a paired change of $-0.0187$ ($-0.0290$ to $-0.0087$). Replacing the linear probe with a fixed shallow gradient-boosting classifier raised Mendelian $C+S+V$ AP to 0.1711, a gain of 0.0689 (0.0174 to 0.1451). This classifier sensitivity was performed after inspection of the data. Its improvement therefore requires independent evaluation, and all topology contrasts remained inconclusive or negative.

\subsection{COSIGT call quality}
We summarized 14,838 published sample--locus outcomes at 265 loci\cite{supp__bolognini2026cosigt} into per-locus targets. The training-set median predicted the primary target, the mean quality-value fraction, with mean absolute error 0.0462, compared with 0.0534 for $C+S$ and 0.0527 (strict) and 0.0538 (one-hop) for $C+S+T$. All topology intervals spanned zero. A fallback that used validation error to choose between each fitted model and the median constant reduced error for $C+S+T$ to 0.0479 and 0.0472 but did not establish a topology benefit. The targets summarize call quality at published, evaluable sample--locus pairs. No genotype calls were changed, and per-variant concordance, genome-wide callability and performance within complex or copy-number-variable strata were not evaluated.

\section{Reconstruction, cross-resource transfer and the degree shortcut}
\label{S-snote:reconstruction}

\subsection{Reconstruction and transfer}
\method recovered hidden connections with region-averaged AP 0.9501 (strict) and 0.9880 (one-hop) on HPRC release~2 and 0.9745 and 0.9868 on HGSVC3 (Supplementary Table~\ref{S-stab:reconstruction}). \section{Training-window scaling}
\label{S-snote:scaling}

To assess sensitivity to the amount of unlabelled training graph, we kept the 52,033-parameter encoder and scorer, the graph, the objective, the folds and the runs fixed and trained on nested, chromosome-balanced subsets containing 12.5\%, 25\%, 50\% and 100\% of training regions. Validation and test candidates were identical across fractions, and the full-data model was retrained under the same conditions rather than taken from earlier results. Realized fractions differ slightly from the nominal values because of rounding within chromosomes and the exclusion of unusable regions.

Fold-pooled reconstruction AP rose from 0.9184 at 12.5\% to 0.9292, 0.9375 and 0.9420 at 25\%, 50\% and 100\% in the strict context, and from 0.9635 to 0.9721, 0.9847 and 0.9900 in the one-hop context (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{S-sfig:scaling}). The 100\%-minus-12.5\% gains were 0.0236 (\CI 0.0216--0.0258) and 0.0265 (0.0253--0.0279). Mean completed epochs ranged from 77.5 to 99.7 across groups, so training compute varied despite the fixed protocol. These measurements describe a learning curve over the tested data fractions.

Biological gains varied across tasks and graph contexts. Relative to the 12.5\% model, the 100\% model changed $C+S+T$ AP by $+0.0035$ (0.0031--0.0039) for strict SV classification and $-0.0015$ ($-0.0028$ to $-0.0007$) for one-hop SV classification; cCRE AP improved by 0.0009 (0.0004--0.0015) in the one-hop context and was inconclusive in the strict context ($-0.0003$; $-0.0007$ to 0.0000), and intervals for CTCF allele-specific SNVs included zero. The consistent improvement in reconstruction did not extend to biological prediction. A degree-sensitive objective offers one explanation, but these comparisons do not isolate its contribution.

\section{Development of shortcut-resistant objectives}
\label{S-snote:objectives}

\subsection{Rationale}
Under the original objective, every positive endpoint loses a visible connection when the query is hidden, whereas the endpoints of matched negatives need not lose any. We developed two objectives to remove this endpoint-exposure cue (Methods): junction re-pairing, in which positives and negatives are alternative pairings of the same dangling ends, and masked sequence-feature reconstruction, in which connectivity is not a prediction target. Both were developed on fold~A, using its training chromosomes for pretraining and its validation chromosomes (chr2, chr7, chr12, chr17 and chr22) for selection. These chromosomes are the test set of fold~B, which is therefore reported separately in the ongoing chromosome-level replication.

\subsection{Junction re-pairing}
On the development candidates (2,050 pairs in 104 regions, positive fraction 0.50), geometry and degree cues alone were uninformative: endpoint geometry scored 0.5000, visible connection counts 0.5070 and both together 0.5068 (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{S-sfig:objectives}a).

Per-segment inputs that include sequence embeddings reached 0.7097 with logistic regression and 0.7340 with gradient boosting, showing that segment content carries some information about which ends belong together.

In this configuration, encoders passing messages only along incoming connections remained near chance (trained 0.5007 versus untrained 0.5028 with the multilayer scorer), whereas bidirectional encoders achieved higher AP (0.7897 versus 0.6257; with a linear scorer, 0.7400 versus 0.5448). Adding sequence embeddings to the segment inputs gave 0.8407 and 0.8700 for the two scorers, against 0.6794 and 0.6547 for untrained counterparts and 0.7340 for gradient boosting without the graph (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{S-sfig:objectives}b).

These comparisons each use one run on the partition used for model selection and have no confidence intervals. Initial-parameter equivalence is verified for the sequence-conditioned pairs but cannot be verified from the older topology-only checkpoints. The latter therefore remain architecture-matched controls with an initialization-provenance limitation. Under the same matching constraints the strict context retained only 12 eligible regions across all chromosomes, too few for training and validation, so development used the one-hop context only.

Frozen representations from the junction-trained encoder were combined with the original representations and evaluated on the biological tasks under a three-run development criterion, which required the combination to exceed every matched combination containing untrained components after $C+S+H$, and not to fall materially below the better single representation, for both tasks. Only one of three runs met the criterion; the other two failed for cCRE and SV, respectively. Under the predefined rule, we did not advance this encoder to chromosome-level replication.

\subsection{Masked sequence-feature reconstruction}
Twelve pretraining runs (four arms by three runs) and the corresponding frozen probes were completed, and all probes converged. On fold-A validation chromosomes, the trained-minus-untrained AP difference after $C+S+H$ averaged 0.0056 for insertion-versus-deletion classification and 0.0018 for cCREs and was positive in every run (Supplementary Table~\ref{S-stab:development}).

Full encoders also exceeded coordinate-only encoders in every run, by 0.0061 and 0.0024 on average; because the coordinate-only ablation also removes capacity, the comparison combines changes in message passing and capacity. F1 and balanced accuracy were not uniformly better in every run.

Against the junction-trained encoder of the same width, the masked-feature encoder had a mean AP advantage after $C+S+H$ of 0.0019 for insertion-versus-deletion classification and 0.0001 for cCREs, with training budgets that differed (20 versus 10 epochs).

All three runs use the same development chromosomes, so they do not estimate uncertainty across chromosome blocks. Five-fold replication, with fold~B reported separately, and a matched comparison with the original encoder remain incomplete in the evidence reported here. No partial chromosome results are included. The development comparison consequently leaves superiority over the original encoder and external transfer unresolved.

\subsection{Haplotype path extraction}
Haplotype paths were extracted from GBZ graph files\cite{supp__siren2022gbz} and linked to full-resolution segment indexes. Observed traversals were sampled within 5-Mb windows and checked for missing segment handles. No path-specific unobserved connections were generated.

\subsection{TraitGym adaptation}
We preserved TraitGym's matched groups (one positive and nine controls), alleles and chromosome-X examples, and assigned variants to our five chromosome folds rather than the official leave-one-chromosome-out protocol, precluding direct comparison with the benchmark leaderboard. The published allele scores $V$ comprise signed and absolute masked-allele log-likelihood ratios from Nucleotide Transformer 2.5B, joined by verified row order and variant identity.

The classifier sensitivity analysis selected linear regularization from six values on validation data, used a fixed 150-iteration shallow histogram gradient-boosting classifier, and evaluated four validation-selected late-fusion combinations.

\subsection{COSIGT targets}
The primary continuous target was the mean quality-value fraction at each locus; the mean predicted quality value was a secondary target. Seven ridge penalties were compared on validation mean absolute error, with stronger regularization breaking exact ties. The training-median constant, locus length and $H$ were retained as explicit references. The publisher's reference panel excludes its test donors, but donor overlap with the graph used to train \method remains possible.

\subsection{EN-TEx multiplicity}
For allele-specific SNV tasks, all occurrences of a genomic position were assigned to the same chromosome fold, measurement multiplicities were retained as sample weights and every occurrence of a locus received the same prediction. This design tests locus-level susceptibility rather than the direction of imbalance in a given donor.

\subsection{Matched overlap analysis}
Regions were ranked by model prioritization within each chromosome. Each of the top 10\% of regions was matched to five controls on region length, GC content, Umap/Bismap mappability\cite{supp__karimzadeh2018umap}, graph complexity, HPRC variant density and distance to the nearest GENCODE v50 gene\cite{supp__frankish2023gencode}. Signal sets comprised chromosome-8 eQTL and sQTL positions\cite{supp__lappalainen2013transcriptome}, all-trait GWAS Catalog positions and positions filtered for Parkinson's disease\cite{supp__sollis2023gwas}.

The QTL analysis was a prespecified chromosome-8 pilot: only the chromosome-8 summary files were retrieved, after checking download size and local storage, and the chromosome was not selected on the basis of enrichment. Inference used 10,000 one-sided matched permutations and 2,000 bootstrap replicates for intervals.

\subsection{Recorded settings}
For each principal analysis, we recorded the model configuration and version, chromosome partition, run identifier, graph context, excluded candidate pairs, predictions and metrics. Aggregate summaries were produced only after every prescribed fold--run combination was complete.
